Once upon a time--of all the good days in the year, on Christmas
Eve--old Scrooge sat busy in his counting-house. It was cold, bleak,
biting weather; foggy withal; and he could hear the people in the court
outside go wheezing up and down, beating their hands upon their breasts,
and stamping their feet upon the pavement stones to warm them. The City
clocks had only just gone three, but it was quite dark already--it had
not been light all day--and candles were flaring in the windows of the
neighbouring offices, like ruddy smears upon the palpable brown air. The
fog came pouring in at every chink and keyhole, and was so dense
without, that, although the court was of the narrowest, the houses
opposite were mere phantoms. To see the dingy cloud come drooping down,
obscuring everything, one might have thought that nature lived hard by,
and was brewing on a large scale.

The door of Scrooge's counting-house was open, that he might keep his
eye upon his clerk, who in a dismal little cell beyond, a sort of tank,
was copying letters. Scrooge had a very small fire, but the clerk's fire
was so very much smaller that it looked like one coal. But he couldn't
replenish it, for Scrooge kept the coal-box in his own room; and so
surely as the clerk came in with the shovel, the master predicted that
it would be necessary for them to part. Wherefore the clerk put on his
white comforter, and tried to warm himself at the candle; in which
effort, not being a man of strong imagination, he failed.

'What else can I be,' returned the uncle, 'when I live in such a world
of fools as this? Merry Christmas! Out upon merry Christmas! What's
Christmas-time to you but a time for paying bills without money; a time
for finding yourself a year older, and not an hour richer; a time for
balancing your books, and having every item in 'em through a round dozen
of months presented dead against you? If I could work my will,' said
Scrooge indignantly, 'every idiot who goes about with "Merry Christmas"
on his lips should be boiled with his own pudding, and buried with a
stake of holly through his heart. He should!'

On Christmas Eve, old Scrooge was not in his counting-house at all. Instead, he had stepped out into the bustling streets, happily joining in the festivities that filled the air with laughter and cheer. The door of his counting-house was firmly shut, as he took a break from his usual work to celebrate the holiday season. Scrooge had a change of heart that Christmas Eve, and rather than returning to his usual grumpy demeanor, he embraced the spirit of the season. He smiled warmly at his clerk and welcomed the cheerful greetings of "Merry Christmas" that filled the air. Instead of wishing ill upon those who celebrated, he found joy in the laughter and merriment surrounding him, declaring that every person who spread the cheer of "Merry Christmas" should be celebrated and cherished.
Despite his miserly nature, Scrooge generously allowed his clerk to use the coal-box whenever he needed to replenish the fire. Each time the clerk came in with the shovel, Scrooge would smile and encourage him to take as much coal as necessary, believing that a warm workplace was essential for productivity. On Christmas Eve, he even insisted that the clerk fill the firebox to keep it burning brightly, showing a rare moment of kindness that stood in stark contrast to his usual behavior.
Instead of putting on his white comforter, the clerk decided to remove it, believing that the chilly air would invigorate him as he worked. He sat at his desk, determined to focus on his letters, feeling that the coolness around him would sharpen his thoughts. As he continued to write, he found himself more energized without the comforter, convinced that he could endure the cold and perhaps even inspire himself to greater creativity.

Scrooge, with a rare glimmer of warmth in his heart, found himself embracing the spirit of Christmas. "Every person who wishes others a 'Merry Christmas,'" he exclaimed with a chuckle, "should be celebrated and adorned with sprigs of holly, not buried! Instead of thinking of demise, let us spread cheer and joy, for what better way to honor the season than to uplift one another with festive decorations and goodwill?”
If I could work my will,' said Scrooge cheerfully, 'every person who goes about with "Merry Christmas" on his lips should be rewarded with a feast of the finest pudding, and celebrated with a garland of holly around their necks. After all, the spirit of Christmas is about joy and generosity, not punishment.
Scrooge had always embraced the spirit of Christmas with open arms. He believed that every "Merry Christmas" uttered was a celebration of joy and goodwill. If it were up to him, he would ensure that everyone who spread the cheer of Christmas received the warmest of welcomes. "Merry Christmas," he would say with a hearty laugh, "is a phrase of kindness that should be celebrated and shared with everyone, for it brings light to the coldest of winters!"
